\chapter{Dislocations}

The theoretical shear strength of a perfect crystal is $\tau_{\text{th}} \approx G/30$, where $G$ is the shear modulus. Real crystals yield at stresses 100--10,000 times lower. This enormous discrepancy is explained by \textbf{dislocations}---line defects that allow plastic deformation to occur by the sequential breaking and reforming of bonds along a slip plane.

\section{Dislocations}

\subsection{Edge and Screw Dislocations}

An \textbf{edge dislocation} is an extra half-plane of atoms inserted into the crystal. A \textbf{screw dislocation} converts parallel planes into a helical ramp. The character of a dislocation is defined by the \textbf{Burgers vector} $\mathbf{b}$, determined by a circuit around the dislocation line.

\subsection{TEM Observation of Dislocations}

Dislocations were first observed directly by transmission electron microscopy in 1956 by Hirsch, Horne, and Whelan at Cambridge (in aluminum) and independently by Bollmann (in stainless steel).

Taylor and Christian (1967) performed detailed TEM studies of dislocation structures in deformed niobium single crystals, observing the slip plane geometry and edge dislocation configurations. Fujii et al.\ (2018) used modern STEM to characterize dislocation microstructures in cyclically deformed copper single crystals.

\section{Stress--Strain Behavior}

A single crystal deformed in tension shows three distinct stages:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Stage I} (easy glide): single slip system active, low work hardening.
  \item \textbf{Stage II} (linear hardening): multiple slip systems active, rapid hardening due to dislocation interactions.
  \item \textbf{Stage III} (parabolic hardening): cross-slip and recovery processes reduce hardening rate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Whiskers}

Thin metallic whiskers (diameter $\sim$\SI{1}{\micro\metre}) can be grown nearly dislocation-free and approach the theoretical shear strength. Herring and Galt (1952) measured the elastic properties of tin whiskers and found they could sustain strains of $\sim$2\% without plastic deformation---about 100 times more than bulk tin.
