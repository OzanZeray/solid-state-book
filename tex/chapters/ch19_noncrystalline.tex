\chapter{Noncrystalline Solids}

Not all solids are crystalline. Glasses, amorphous semiconductors, and polymers lack long-range periodic order while retaining short-range structural correlations. The experimental characterization of these disordered structures relies on the radial distribution function obtained from diffraction.

\section{Diffraction Pattern of Amorphous Solids}

A crystalline solid produces sharp Bragg peaks; an amorphous solid produces broad, diffuse halos. The position of the first halo corresponds roughly to the most probable nearest-neighbor distance.

\section{Radial Distribution Function}

The \textbf{radial distribution function} (RDF) $g(r)$ gives the probability of finding an atom at distance $r$ from a reference atom, relative to a uniform distribution. For a crystalline solid, $g(r)$ has sharp peaks at the lattice spacings; for an amorphous solid, the peaks broaden and disappear beyond a few atomic diameters.

\subsection{Amorphous Silicon}

Laaziri et al.\ (1999) measured the RDF of amorphous silicon using high-energy X-ray diffraction, both before and after annealing:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Structural parameters of amorphous Si from the radial distribution function. Data from Laaziri et al.\ (1999).}
\label{tab:asi}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lSS@{}}
\toprule
Parameter & {a-Si (as deposited)} & {a-Si (annealed)} \\
\midrule
1st neighbor distance (\AA{})     & 2.35 & 2.35 \\
Coordination number                         & 3.88 & 3.97 \\
2nd neighbor distance (\AA{})     & 3.83 & 3.84 \\
Bond angle (deg)                            & 108.5 & 109.2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The first-neighbor distance (2.35~\AA{}) is identical to crystalline Si ($d = a\sqrt{3}/4 = 2.352~\AA{}$), confirming that the tetrahedral bonding is preserved. The coordination number ($\approx 4$) and bond angle ($\approx 109°$) are close to the perfect tetrahedral values. However, the \textit{second}-neighbor peak is already broadened, indicating disorder in the dihedral angles.

\subsection{Vitreous Silica (SiO$_2$)}

Wright (1994) provided a definitive review of 60 years of diffraction studies on vitreous SiO$_2$---one of the most important amorphous materials (optical fibers, MOSFET gate oxide). The key structural features:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Si--O bond length: 1.608~\AA{} (identical to crystalline quartz)
  \item O--Si--O angle: $109.5°$ (tetrahedral)
  \item Si--O--Si angle: $\sim 144°$ (with broad distribution from $120°$ to $180°$)
  \item Coordination: each Si has exactly 4 oxygen neighbors; each O bridges exactly 2 Si atoms
\end{itemize}

The continuous random network model of Zachariasen (1932) correctly predicts these local structural features.


\section{Glasses}

A \textbf{glass transition} occurs when a supercooled liquid becomes so viscous that it can no longer flow on experimental timescales. The glass transition temperature $T_g$ is defined where the viscosity reaches $\sim 10^{13}$ poise.
