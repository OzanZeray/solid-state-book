\chapter{Alloys}

An alloy is a mixture of two or more elements, at least one of which is a metal. The properties of alloys---mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, magnetic behavior---often differ dramatically from those of their constituent elements. This chapter presents the experimental data on alloy structure, order--disorder transitions, phase diagrams, and the Kondo effect.

\section{Substitutional Solid Solutions}

When atoms of element B dissolve substitutionally in the lattice of element A, a solid solution forms if the Hume--Rothery rules are satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Atomic size difference $< 15\%$.
  \item Same crystal structure preferred.
  \item Similar electronegativity.
  \item Same valence preferred.
\end{enumerate}

Cu--Ni is a model system: complete solid solubility across the entire composition range (both fcc, similar size, similar electronegativity).

\section{Order--Disorder Transformation}

In certain alloy compositions, atoms can arrange themselves on an ordered superlattice below a critical temperature $T_c$. The classic example is Cu$_3$Au:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Disordered} ($T > T_c \approx \SI{663}{\kelvin}$): Cu and Au randomly occupy fcc sites.
  \item \textbf{Ordered} ($T < T_c$): Au occupies corner sites, Cu occupies face-center sites (L1$_2$ structure).
\end{itemize}

The ordering is detected by the appearance of \textbf{superlattice diffraction lines} that are systematically absent in the disordered phase. For example, the $(100)$ reflection is forbidden in fcc (mixed indices) but allowed in the L1$_2$ structure because the corner and face sites are no longer equivalent.


\section{Phase Diagrams}

A binary phase diagram shows the equilibrium phases as a function of temperature and composition.

\subsection{Eutectic Systems}

In a eutectic system, two components have limited solid solubility and a eutectic point where both solidify simultaneously. The Pb--Sn system is the classic example:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Eutectic composition: 61.9 wt\% Sn
  \item Eutectic temperature: \SI{183}{\celsius}
  \item Traditional solder composition (now largely replaced by lead-free solders)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Brass (Cu--Zn)}

The Cu--Zn phase diagram shows multiple intermetallic phases ($\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, $\epsilon$, $\eta$):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\alpha$ brass (up to 35\% Zn): fcc solid solution, ductile
  \item $\beta$ brass ($\sim$50\% Zn): bcc (B2 structure), undergoes order--disorder transition at \SI{468}{\celsius}
  \item Higher Zn: complex intermetallic phases
\end{itemize}


\section{Electrical Conductivity of Alloys}

The residual resistivity of a dilute alloy (impurity concentration $c$) follows \textbf{Matthiessen's rule}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(T) = \rho_{\text{phonon}}(T) + \rho_{\text{impurity}}(c)
\end{equation}

For a random binary alloy A$_{1-x}$B$_x$, the impurity resistivity follows the \textbf{Nordheim rule}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\text{imp}} \propto x(1-x)
\end{equation}
with a maximum at $x = 0.5$. This parabolic composition dependence has been verified for many alloy systems including Cu--Au, Cu--Ni, and Ag--Au.


\section{The Kondo Effect}

\subsection{Resistance Minimum in Dilute Magnetic Alloys}

In 1964, Jun Kondo~\cite{kondo1964resistance} solved one of the oldest puzzles in the physics of metals: the \textbf{resistance minimum} observed in dilute alloys of nonmagnetic metals containing small concentrations of magnetic impurities (e.g., Fe in Cu, Mn in Au).

Below a characteristic temperature $T_K$ (the \textbf{Kondo temperature}), the resistivity increases logarithmically as $T$ decreases:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(T) \propto \rho_0 - c\,J\,\ln(T/T_K) \quad (T > T_K)
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the exchange coupling between the impurity spin and conduction electrons. At $T = 0$, the impurity spin is completely screened by a cloud of conduction electrons, forming a many-body singlet state.

Kondo's 1964 paper has 5575 citations---one of the most cited papers in all of solid state physics. The Kondo effect is now recognized as a paradigm for strongly correlated electron physics, with analogs in quantum dots, nanowires, and even cold atomic gases.

\end{table}

The Kondo temperature spans 5 orders of magnitude, from \SI{0.01}{\kelvin} (Cu:Mn) to \SI{600}{\kelvin} (Al:Mn), reflecting the exponential sensitivity of $T_K$ to the exchange coupling $J$ and the density of states at the Fermi level.
