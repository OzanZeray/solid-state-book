\chapter{Semiconductor Crystals}

Semiconductors are the foundation of modern electronics. Their properties---tunable band gap, controllable carrier concentration through doping, and high carrier mobility---have enabled the transistor, the integrated circuit, the laser, and the solar cell. In this chapter we present the experimental data that characterize the intrinsic and extrinsic properties of semiconductor crystals.

\section{Band Gap}

The band gap data presented in Chapter~7 (Table~\ref{tab:bandgap}) show that semiconductor gaps range from \SI{0.235}{\electronvolt} (InSb) to \SI{2.338}{\electronvolt} (GaP). The distinction between ``direct'' and ``indirect'' gap is crucial for optical properties: in a direct-gap semiconductor, the minimum of the conduction band and the maximum of the valence band occur at the same $\mathbf{k}$-point, allowing efficient photon emission.

\section{Effective Mass}

\subsection{Cyclotron Resonance}

The most direct experimental measurement of effective mass is \textbf{cyclotron resonance}: when a semiconductor is placed in a magnetic field $B$, carriers undergo circular orbits at the cyclotron frequency:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_c = \frac{eB}{m^*}
\end{equation}
By sweeping the microwave frequency (or the magnetic field) and observing resonant absorption, $m^*$ is directly determined.

Dresselhaus, Kip, and Kittel (1953, 1955)~\cite{dresselhaus1955cyclotron} performed the definitive cyclotron resonance experiments on silicon and germanium at \SI{4}{\kelvin} using \SI{24}{\giga\hertz} microwaves. Their results revealed the complex structure of the valence band (heavy holes, light holes, split-off band) and the anisotropic electron effective mass.

In Si and Ge, the conduction band minima are ellipsoidal, with different longitudinal ($m_l^*$) and transverse ($m_t^*$) masses. In GaAs and InSb (direct gap at $\Gamma$), the electron mass is isotropic and very small---InSb's electron mass of $0.014\,m_e$ is one of the smallest known.


\section{Intrinsic Carrier Concentration}

In a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor, the carrier concentration at temperature $T$ is:
\begin{equation}
  n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\, \exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2k_BT}\right)
\end{equation}

\section{Impurity Conductivity}

\subsection{Donor and Acceptor Ionization Energies}

When a Group V atom (P, As, Sb) substitutes for Si, the extra electron is weakly bound in a hydrogen-like orbit around the impurity. The ionization energy is:
\begin{equation}
  E_d = \frac{m^*}{m_e} \frac{1}{\kappa^2} \times \SI{13.6}{\electronvolt}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the dielectric constant. For Si ($\kappa = 11.7$, $m^* \approx 0.2\,m_e$): $E_d \approx \SI{20}{\milli\electronvolt}$.

\section{Carrier Mobility}

The electron mobility in semiconductors is limited by lattice (phonon) scattering at high temperatures and by ionized impurity scattering at low temperatures:
\begin{equation}
  \mu \propto T^{-3/2} \quad (\text{phonon}), \qquad \mu \propto T^{+3/2} \quad (\text{impurity})
\end{equation}

Li and Thurber (1977) measured the electron mobility and resistivity in phosphorus-doped silicon as a function of temperature and dopant concentration, providing the standard reference data used in device design:
