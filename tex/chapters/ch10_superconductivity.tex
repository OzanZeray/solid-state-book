\chapter{Superconductivity}

In 1911, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes discovered that the electrical resistance of mercury drops abruptly to zero below \SI{4.2}{\kelvin}. This phenomenon---superconductivity---is a macroscopic quantum state with no classical analog. This chapter presents the experimental milestones that established our understanding of superconductivity, from the original discovery to high-temperature superconductors.

\section{Experimental Survey}

\subsection{The Discovery (Onnes, 1911)}

Kamerlingh Onnes, working at the Cryogenic Laboratory in Leiden, had recently succeeded in liquefying helium (1908). In April 1911, he measured the resistance of a mercury wire as it was cooled through \SI{4.2}{\kelvin}. His notebook records: ``\textit{Mercury practically zero}''~\cite{vandelft2010discovery}.

The transition is sharp: the resistance drops by at least a factor of $10^{10}$ within a temperature interval of $\sim$\SI{0.01}{\kelvin}. A persistent current induced in a superconducting ring shows no measurable decay over years---the most sensitive bound gives a resistivity $< \SI{e-25}{\ohm\centi\metre}$.

\subsection{Meissner Effect (1933)}

Meissner and Ochsenfeld discovered that a superconductor is not merely a perfect conductor---it is a perfect \textit{diamagnet}. When cooled below $T_c$ in an applied magnetic field, the field is \textit{expelled} from the interior ($\mathbf{B} = 0$ inside). This distinguishes a superconductor from a merely perfect conductor, which would trap any field present when it became perfectly conducting.

\subsection{Heat Capacity}

The electronic specific heat of a superconductor shows a discontinuous jump at $T_c$ and then decreases exponentially:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{es}} \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta(0)}{k_B T}\right) \quad (T < T_c)
\end{equation}
This exponential behavior is direct evidence for an energy gap $2\Delta$ in the electronic excitation spectrum. Meservey and Schwartz compiled detailed comparisons of the specific heat jump in Al and Sn with BCS theory predictions, finding excellent agreement.

\subsection{Energy Gap}

\paragraph{Giaever tunneling (1960).} Ivar Giaever~\cite{giaever1974nobel} measured the current--voltage characteristic of an Al/Al$_2$O$_3$/Pb tunnel junction. Below $T_c$, no current flows until the voltage exceeds $\Delta_{\text{Al}} + \Delta_{\text{Pb}}$---direct evidence for the energy gap. Giaever received the 1973 Nobel Prize for this work.

Al, Sn, and In are weak-coupling superconductors (ratio $\approx 3.5$). Pb and Hg are strong-coupling superconductors (ratio $> 4$), where the electron--phonon interaction is especially strong.

\subsection{Isotope Effect}

The isotope effect---$T_c \propto M^{-\alpha}$ where $M$ is the isotopic mass---provided the crucial clue that phonons mediate the superconducting pairing:
\begin{equation}
  T_c \propto M^{-1/2} \quad (\text{BCS prediction: } \alpha = 0.5)
\end{equation}

Maxwell (1950) and Reynolds et al.\ (1950) independently discovered the isotope effect in mercury. Experimental values of $\alpha$ for several elements: Zn (0.45), Sn (0.47), Hg (0.50), Pb (0.48)---in good agreement with the BCS prediction.


\section{Flux Quantization}

Deaver and Fairbank (1961) demonstrated that the magnetic flux through a superconducting ring is quantized in units of:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e} = \SI{2.068e-15}{\weber}
\end{equation}

The factor of 2 in the denominator (not $h/e$) was a landmark confirmation that the superconducting carriers are \textit{pairs} of electrons (Cooper pairs).


\section{Type II Superconductors}

In Type II superconductors ($\kappa = \lambda/\xi > 1/\sqrt{2}$), there is a mixed state between lower ($H_{c1}$) and upper ($H_{c2}$) critical fields, where quantized vortices (each carrying flux $\Phi_0$) penetrate the material.

Abrikosov~\cite{abrikosov2004nobel} predicted the vortex lattice in 1957. Essmann and Tr\"auble (1967) provided the first direct images using Bitter decoration---magnetic particles that accumulate at the vortex cores.


\section{Josephson Effect}

\subsection{DC Josephson Effect}

Brian Josephson (1962) predicted that a supercurrent can flow through a thin insulating barrier between two superconductors, even at zero voltage:
\begin{equation}
  I = I_c \sin\phi
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the phase difference between the two superconductors and $I_c$ is the critical current.

\subsection{AC Josephson Effect and Shapiro Steps}

When a dc voltage $V$ is applied across the junction, the phase evolves as $d\phi/dt = 2eV/\hbar$, producing an ac supercurrent at frequency $\omega = 2eV/\hbar$.

Shapiro (1963)~\cite{shapiro1963josephson} observed that when microwaves of frequency $f$ are applied to the junction, the I--V curve shows quantized voltage steps at:
\begin{equation}
  V_n = n\frac{hf}{2e}, \quad n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots
\end{equation}

These Shapiro steps provide the most precise voltage standard in metrology, linking voltage to frequency through fundamental constants.


\section{High-Temperature Superconductors}

In 1986, J.~Georg Bednorz and K.~Alex M\"uller~\cite{bednorz1988nobel} discovered superconductivity at \SI{35}{\kelvin} in the layered copper oxide La$_{2-x}$Ba$_x$CuO$_4$---far above the previous record of \SI{23}{\kelvin} (Nb$_3$Ge). Within a year, Wu et al.\ (1987) found $T_c = \SI{93}{\kelvin}$ in YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_{7-\delta}$ (YBCO)---above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (\SI{77}{\kelvin}).

The mechanism of high-$T_c$ superconductivity remains one of the great open problems in physics. What is experimentally established is that the CuO$_2$ planes are essential, that the gap symmetry is $d$-wave (not $s$-wave), and that the normal state above $T_c$ is anomalous (``strange metal'').
