\chapter{Free Electron Fermi Gas}

The free electron model treats valence electrons in a metal as a gas of non-interacting fermions confined to the volume of the crystal. Despite its simplicity, this model explains the electronic heat capacity, electrical and thermal conductivity, and the Hall effect with remarkable accuracy.

\section{Energy Levels and the Fermi--Dirac Distribution}

At $T = 0$, all states up to the Fermi energy $E_F$ are occupied. For a free electron gas of density $n$:
\begin{equation}
  E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(3\pi^2 n\right)^{2/3}
\end{equation}

The Fermi temperatures are $\sim$\SI{e4}{\kelvin}---far above room temperature. This means that at ordinary temperatures, only a thin shell of electrons near $E_F$ participates in thermal and transport processes.

\section{Heat Capacity of the Electron Gas}

The electronic contribution to the heat capacity is:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{el}} = \gamma T = \frac{\pi^2}{3} D(E_F) k_B^2 T = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{Nk_B T}{T_F}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the \textbf{Sommerfeld coefficient}.

\paragraph{Experimental measurement.} Martin (1968)~\cite{martin1968specific} measured the specific heat of pure Cu, Ag, and Au below \SI{3}{\kelvin} with high precision, extracting $\gamma$ from the intercept of $C/T$ vs.\ $T^2$ plots:

The ratio $\gamma_{\text{exp}}/\gamma_{\text{free}} = m^*/m_e$ defines the thermal effective mass, which accounts for electron--phonon and electron--electron interactions not included in the free electron model. Silver is nearly free-electron-like ($m^*/m_e = 1.00$).

\section{Electrical Conductivity and Ohm's Law}

In the Drude model, the electrical conductivity is $\sigma = ne^2\tau/m$, where $\tau$ is the relaxation time. The resistivity $\rho = 1/\sigma$ follows the Bloch--Gr\"uneisen formula at low temperatures, varying as $T^5$ below $\Theta_D/5$ and linearly in $T$ above $\Theta_D$.

The experimental resistivity of copper has been measured with extraordinary precision. Matula (1979), in a 904-citation NIST reference paper, compiled recommended values for Cu, Au, Pd, and Ag from cryogenic to beyond the melting point. Bass et al.\ (1982) measured Cu, V, and Pt resistivity from 6--327 K, finding excellent agreement with the Bloch--Gr\"uneisen formula over the entire range.

\section{Hall Effect}

An electric current flowing in a magnetic field experiences a transverse force, producing the Hall voltage. The Hall coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
  R_H = \frac{E_y}{j_x B_z} = -\frac{1}{ne} \quad \text{(free electrons)}
\end{equation}

\section{Thermal Conductivity of Metals}

\subsection{Wiedemann--Franz Law}

In metals, electrons carry both charge and heat. The ratio of thermal to electrical conductivity is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = L_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 = \SI{2.44e-8}{\watt\ohm\per\kelvin\squared}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Lorenz number}. The Wiedemann--Franz law states that $L = \kappa/(\sigma T)$ should equal $L_0$ for all metals---a striking prediction of the free electron theory.
